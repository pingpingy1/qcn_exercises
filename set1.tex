\section*{Problem 1}
The benefits of applying quantum technology come not from communication speeds,
but rather the usefulness of sharing quantum states itself.
\textbf{Quantum key distribution} (QKD) is one of them:
to ensure computational security, we use the quantum mechanical principle that
perfectly discriminating non-orthogonal states is impossible.
Another is \textbf{quantum teleportation}, where unknown states can be sent far away,
enabling distributed quantum computing systems.
In both of these cases, qubits and ebits (entanglement) are the crucial quantum resources.

\section*{Problem 2}
For a qubit in a pure state
\[
    \ket{\psi} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\ket{0} + \frac{1}{2}\ket{1},
\]
measurement probabilities can be determined using the Born rule:
\[
    P(0) = \left|\braket{0|\psi}\right|^2 = \frac{3}{4},\quad
    P(1) = \left|\braket{1|\psi}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{4}.
\]

\section*{Problem 3}
An unknown quantum state cannot be simply read, copied, and retransmitted as classical information could.
This is called the \emph{no-cloning theorem}, and it stems from the fact that
quantum measurement processes are destructive, i.e., measuring a system
collapses its state into one of the measurement bases.
Thus, quantum communication requires \emph{quantum repeaters},
which help with retaining coherence in long-distance entanglements without amplifying signals.

\section*{Problem 4}
\textbf{No}, local measurements on Alice's side cannot send any information to Bob.
While entanglemnt allows for nonlocal \emph{correlations} between separate measurements,
it cannot create nonlocal \emph{causations} between them.
This can be readily seen in the reduced density matrix formulation,
wherein Alice's local operations (unitary or measurement) does not affect Bob's reduced density matrix.
Therefore, no matter what Alice does with her qubit, Bob's measurement outcomes are unaffected.

\section*{Problem 5}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Photon loss:}
        Photons are probabilistically absorbed or scattered by a realistic fiber optic cable,
        the likelihood of which increases with the distance travelled.
        Quantum repeaters mitigate this by breaking up the distance into shorter segments,
        swapping entanglements with each other and the endpoints.

    \item \textbf{Decoherence:}
        Photons could interact with their environments, losing the coherent entangled state.
        This is why quantum channels are typically in vacuum and low temperatures:
        to reduce the probability of such decohering interactions.

    \item \textbf{Probabilistic success:}
        Quantum repeater-based schemes often rely on random success events,
        which introduce temporal delays and could exacerbate the decoherence problem.
        One can use quantum memories with long lifetimes to hold onto a quantum state
        while waiting for the protocol to succeed.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Problem 6}
The Bloch vector of a pure state $\ket{\psi}$ is found as
\[
    \vec{r} = (\braket{X}, \braket{Y}, \braket{Z}).
\]
Or equivalently, in the computational basis $\ket{\psi} = \alpha\ket{0} + \beta\ket{1}$,
one can find:
\[
    \vec{r} = (2\Re\{\alpha^*\beta\}, 2\Im\{\alpha^*\beta\}, |\alpha|^2- |\beta|^2).
\]
Therefore, we can find the corresponding Bloch vectors as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\ket{0} \Rightarrow \vec{r}=(0, 0, 1)$
    \item $\ket{1} \Rightarrow \vec{r}=(0, 0, -1)$
    \item $\ket{+} \Rightarrow \vec{r}=(1, 0, 0)$
\end{itemize}

\section*{Problem 7}
\[
    \rho
    = \ketbra{\psi}{\psi}
    = \frac{1}{2} \left(
        \ketbra{0}{0} - i\ketbra{0}{1} + i\ketbra{1}{0} + \ketbra{1}{1}
    \right)
    \doteq \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\]
\[
    \Rightarrow \rho^2 = \rho
    \Rightarrow \tr(\rho^2) = 1
\]
This is consistent with the fact that $\ket{\psi}$ is a pure state.

\section*{Problem 8}
\[
    \rho_+ := \ketbra{+}{+} = \frac{1}{2} \left(
        \ketbra{0}{0} + \ketbra{0}{1} + \ketbra{1}{0} + \ketbra{1}{1}
    \right)
    \doteq \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\]
\[
    \rho_{\text{mix}} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \ketbra{0}{0} + \ketbra{1}{1} \right)
    \doteq \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\]
The difference lies in the off-diagonal elements, which correlate with how coherent the state is.
This is manifestly evident in the $X$-basis measurement, where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $P_+(+)=1$ and $P_+(-) = 0$, but
    \item $P_{\text{mix}}(+) = P_{\text{mix}}(-) = \frac{1}{2}$.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Problem 11}
A projective measurement $\{P_m\}$ is defined via the following conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Each operator is an orthogonal projection:
        $\forall m.\; P_m = P_m^2 = P_m^\dagger$

    \item The ranges of each projection are pairwise orthogonal:
        $\forall m, n.\; P_m P_n = \delta_{mn} P_n$

    \item The projections are collectively complete:
        $\sum_m P_m = I$
\end{itemize}

\section*{Problem 12}
For fun, let us use the density matrix formalism for this problem:
\[
    \rho = \ketbra{\psi}{\psi} \doteq \begin{pmatrix}
        \frac{1}{4} & \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} \\ \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} & \frac{3}{4}
    \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The computational basis measurement can be formulated as a PVM:
\[
    \Pi_0 = \ketbra{0}{0},\quad
    \Pi_1 = \ketbra{1}{1}.
\]
The Born rule allows us to find the probabilities of each measurement outcome:
\[
    P(0) = \tr\left(\Pi_0 \rho\right) = \frac{1}{4},\quad
    P(1) = \tr\left(\Pi_1 \rho\right) = \frac{3}{4}.
\]

\section*{Problem 13}
The post-measurement state can be calculated in the PVM formalism as well:
\[
    \rho_0 = \frac{\Pi_0 \rho \Pi_0}{P(0)} = \ketbra{0}{0}
    \Rightarrow \ket{\psi}_0 = \ket{0}
\]
\[
    \rho_1 = \frac{\Pi_1 \rho \Pi_1}{P(1)} = \ketbra{1}{1}
    \Rightarrow \ket{\psi}_1 = \ket{1}
\]

\section*{Problem 14}
A POVM is defined to be Hermitian, positive semi-definite operators that sum to unity.
The given operators form a POVM set:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $E_0^\dagger = E_0,\;
        E_0 \geq 0\; (\because \text{eigenvalues: } \frac{2}{3}, 0)$

    \item $E_1 = \frac{1}{3}\ketbra{0}{0} + \ketbra{1}{1}$, so
        $E_1^\dagger = E_1,\;
        E_1 \geq 0\; (\because \text{eigenvalues: } \frac{1}{3}, 1),\;
        E_0 + E_1 = I$
\end{itemize}
For the state $\ket{0}$, the outcome probabilities are:
\[
    P(E_0) = \tr\left(E_0\ketbra{0}{0}\right) = \braket{0 | E_0 | 0} = \frac{2}{3},\quad
    P(E_1) = \tr\left(E_1\ketbra{0}{0}\right) = \braket{0 | E_1 | 0} = \frac{1}{3}.
\]

\section*{Problem 15}
Suppose Bob uses some POVM $\{E, I - E\}\; (0 \leq E \leq I)$ to discriminate the states,
predicting $\ket{0}$ in the first measurement outcome and $\ket{+}$ in the second.
If Alice prepares $\ket{0}$ with probability $p$ and $\ket{+}$ with probability $1 - p$,
the success probability is given as
\begin{align*}
    P_{\text{success}}
    &= p \braket{0 | E | 0} + (1 - p) \braket{+ | (I - E) | +} \\
    &= 1 - p + \tr\left(\left(p\ketbra{0}{0} + (1-p) \ketbra{+}{+}\right) E\right).
\end{align*}
To maximize the trace in the last expression, $E$ must equal the projection
onto the positive subspace of $p\ketbra{0}{0} + (1-p)\ketbra{+}{+}$.
Even in such a case, however, because $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{+}$ are non-orthogonal,
the success probability cannot reach $1$ for any $E$,
meaning a perfect state discrimination is impossible.
